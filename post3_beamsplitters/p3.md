## 3. A Beam Splitter That Creates Energy

Approved opening:

> In everyday optics, a beam splitter seems simple, a partially reflective mirror that splits an incoming beam into two. In a classical wave description, one might say that part of the electric field is transmitted and part of it is reflected.

Introduce the naive matrix with four positive entries. Do not introduce a sign flip or relative phase yet.

Approved transition:

> Suppose we describe the beam splitter in the simplest possible way, with equal positive coefficients for both paths. With light entering through only one port, everything looks fine: the intensity splits evenly between the two outputs. But if light enters through both ports, this simple model leads to a problem.

Show the naive matrix:

$$
U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then show explicitly:

$$
a=b=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c_1=c_2=\sqrt{2}.
$$

$$
I_{\mathrm{in}}=1^2+1^2=2,
\qquad
I_{\mathrm{out}}=(\sqrt{2})^2+(\sqrt{2})^2=4.
$$

Conclude:

> Our naive beam splitter produces twice as much energy as it receives.

### Deriving unitarity

Do not introduce the word “unitary” as an unexplained requirement.

Start with a general matrix:

$$
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&U_{22}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Require energy conservation for arbitrary inputs.

The column normalization conditions are:

$$
|U_{11}|^2+|U_{21}|^2=1,
$$

$$
|U_{12}|^2+|U_{22}|^2=1.
$$

The interference cross term must vanish:

$$
U_{11}^*U_{12}+U_{21}^*U_{22}=0.
$$

Only after deriving these conditions introduce the term “unitary matrix”.

Then illustrate with:

$$
U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

## 4. Moving Photons Between Modes

Approved transition:

> So, our classical beam splitter is now mathematically consistent. But what happens if we swap classical amplitudes for quantum operators?

Approved opening:

> In quantum optics, we keep the same input and output modes, but replace the classical amplitudes with annihilation and creation operators. The beam splitter still implements a linear transformation, now acting on those operators.

Delete the repetitive introduction referring back to Post 2.

Use the concise description:

> There are two ways to move a photon between the modes. The operator b-dagger a removes a photon from mode a and adds it to mode b. The operator a-dagger b does the reverse.

Represent the operators mathematically as:

$$
\hat b^\dagger\hat a,
\qquad
\hat a^\dagger\hat b.
$$

Remove the discussion of bosonic enhancement, the square-root occupation factors and the detailed exchange amplitudes.

The article already has a separate published post about the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect. Avoid repeating its derivation.

Use a short demonstration:

> Now send one photon into each input port. There are two indistinguishable ways to detect one photon at each output. Because the photons are indistinguishable, quantum mechanics tells us to add the two amplitudes.

$$
A_{11}
=
U_{11}U_{22}+U_{12}U_{21}
=
-\frac12+\frac12=0.
$$

> The amplitudes cancel exactly. The probability of detecting one photon in each output is zero.

Refer to the separate HOM article for details.

### Classical and quantum correlations

Explain that steady classical intensity gives a flat coincidence histogram, while fluctuating classical intensity can produce bunching.

Present the important contrast before deriving the mathematics:

> For classical light, the correlation between detector clicks at zero delay can never be smaller than at any other delay. Surprisingly, quantum light can violate this rule: the probability of detecting two photons close together can actually be suppressed. This phenomenon is known as photon antibunching.

> But where does this classical limit come from?

Then derive the bound concisely.

For stationary classical light:

$$
\bigl(I(t)-I(t+\tau)\bigr)^2\geq 0.
$$

Averaging gives:

$$
\langle I(t)I(t+\tau)\rangle
\leq
\langle I(t)^2\rangle.
$$

Therefore:

$$
g^{(2)}(\tau)\leq g^{(2)}(0).
$$

Also note that classical intensity fluctuations satisfy:

$$
g^{(2)}(0)\geq 1.
$$

A violation is evidence of nonclassical light.

Keep the mathematical explanation short.
